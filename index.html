The Admission Lottery?

A summary of "Variation in Emergency Department Physician Admitting Practices and Subsequent Mortality" for UK Emergency Medicine Clinicians.

The Big Question

ED physicians vary widely in their tendency to admit patients. But does a higher admission rate actually lead to better patient outcomes, specifically lower mortality?

Key Findings

Finding 1: Massive Variation in Admission Rates

Even within the same ED, there was a huge difference in physician admission propensity for the same chief complaint. This variation was not explained by differences in patient comorbidity scores.

Example: Admission Rate for Chest Pain

Finding 2: High Admitters Have More Short Stays

Patients seen by physicians with higher admission rates were significantly more likely to have a short inpatient stay (<24 hours).

For chest pain, patients of high-admitting physicians were ~25% more likely to be discharged in under 24 hours compared to those of low-admitters (31.0% vs 24.8%).

The Crucial Finding: No Mortality Benefit

Treating patients with a physician who had a higher propensity to admit was not associated with any reduction in 30-day mortality.

This held true for mortality at 7 days, 14 days, 90 days, and even 1 year.

30-Day Mortality Rate by Physician Admission Rate

Finding 4: It's a Matter of Practice Style

Physicians with higher admission rates also tended to order more diagnostic tests (both radiology and laboratory), suggesting a more intensive practice pattern overall.

Take-Home Message

This study suggests that a more cautious, higher-admission practice style doesn't necessarily translate to better survival outcomes for common ED presentations. It may, however, contribute to more short-stay admissions, which have implications for patient experience, hospital flow, and healthcare costs.

It's a powerful reminder to critically evaluate our individual admission thresholds and the value each admission provides.
